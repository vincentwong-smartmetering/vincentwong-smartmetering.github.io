Firmware Design Considerations for Long-Life Embedded Devices

An engineering perspective on firmware architecture, power management, communication handling, fault recovery and maintainability for embedded devices expected to operate reliably over extended periods in the field.

By Vincent Wong Yew Hon

Introduction

Firmware in a long-life embedded device has a different responsibility from firmware in a product that is frequently connected to a user, regularly charged or easily returned for service.

Remote sensors, smart meters, industrial monitoring devices and other connected products may be expected to operate for many years with limited physical access. Once deployed, a firmware defect can become difficult and expensive to correct. Excessive power consumption may shorten the service interval, while poor recovery behaviour can leave a device unavailable until a technician visits the site.

These products therefore need more than code that performs the required functions. They need firmware designed around the complete operating life of the device: how it starts, measures, stores information, communicates, handles abnormal conditions, receives updates and remains supportable over time.

Long-life firmware design is consequently a system engineering activity. It must consider the hardware platform, battery characteristics, communication environment, data requirements, security expectations and practical maintenance model together.

1. Start with the Operating-Life Requirements

Firmware architecture should begin with a clear understanding of how the device is expected to operate in the field.

A battery-powered sensor that reports once a day has a different operating profile from a device that measures frequently, maintains a persistent network connection or performs local analytics. A product installed in an accessible indoor environment may also have a different maintenance model from one installed in a buried chamber, remote pipeline or high-rise meter room.

Before selecting an architecture, define the operating assumptions:

  • Service-life expectation: Intended operating period and planned battery or device replacement interval.
  • Measurement profile: Measurement frequency, resolution, processing requirements and acceptable delays.
  • Communication profile: Reporting interval, payload size, network availability and acknowledgement requirements.
  • Power constraints: Battery chemistry, usable capacity, peak-current capability, temperature range and voltage limits.
  • Data-retention requirements: How much information must be retained locally during communication outages.
  • Maintenance model: Whether physical access is practical and whether remote configuration or firmware updates are required.
  • Environmental conditions: Temperature, humidity, vibration and electromagnetic interference.

These requirements should be translated into measurable firmware behaviours. For example, "low power" is not a sufficiently precise requirement. A more useful specification defines sleep current, measurement duration, reporting frequency, communication retry limits and behaviour when the network is unavailable.

A realistic operating profile must also include exceptional events. Repeated network registration attempts, firmware updates and fault recovery can materially affect average energy consumption, even when they occur infrequently.

2. Build a Modular and Predictable Firmware Architecture

Long-life products benefit from firmware that separates hardware-specific functions from application behaviour. This makes the software easier to test, review and maintain when components or requirements change.

A practical architecture may be organised into several logical layers:

  • Hardware abstraction and drivers: Control microcontroller peripherals, sensors, memory devices, communication modules and power switches.
  • Device services: Provide reusable functions for measurement, timekeeping, storage, communication, diagnostics and power management.
  • Application logic: Coordinate the device operating sequence and implement product-specific functions.
  • Data management: Validate, queue, persist and retrieve measurements, events and configuration information.
  • Fault management: Detect abnormal conditions, record relevant information and coordinate recovery actions.
  • Update and security services: Manage firmware integrity, version compatibility, configuration and authorised maintenance operations.

The objective is not to introduce unnecessary software complexity. A small microcontroller may not require a real-time operating system or a large middleware stack. A well-structured event-driven loop or state machine may be sufficient.

The architecture should match the available processing resources, memory, timing requirements and team capability. The important principle is to make responsibilities and interfaces explicit.

Use Explicit Operating States

State-based design is particularly useful for devices that follow a repeatable operating cycle. Typical states may include sleep, measurement, data processing, storage, communication preparation, network registration, transmission and recovery.

Each state should define its entry conditions, actions, exit conditions, timeout behaviour and permitted transitions. This helps prevent unexpected interactions, such as a communication task keeping the device awake indefinitely or a measurement task starting while a required peripheral is still powering up.

State transitions should also be observable during development and testing. A clear state model makes it easier to identify where a device is spending time and energy when its field behaviour differs from expectations.

3. Treat Power Management as a Firmware Function

Battery life is often discussed as a hardware selection issue, but firmware has a direct influence on how much energy the device consumes over its operating life.

Average current depends on both the current drawn in each operating state and the time spent in that state:

Iavg = Σ (Ii × ti) / T

Where Ii is the current consumed in a particular state, ti is the duration of that state and T is the total observation period.

This is an engineering estimate. Actual battery life also depends on battery ageing, temperature, self-discharge, pulse capability, regulator losses, voltage cut-off limits and the real distribution of operating events.

Control the Complete Operating Cycle

Firmware should minimise unnecessary active time and ensure that peripherals are placed into appropriate low-power states when they are not needed.

  • Use low-power sleep modes where the application permits them.
  • Disable or power-gate sensors, communication modules and external circuits when they are not required.
  • Minimise unnecessary polling and use interrupts or scheduled wake-ups where appropriate.
  • Keep processing and memory activity proportionate to the required function.
  • Review start-up delays and peripheral settling time so that hardware is not powered earlier or longer than necessary.
  • Measure the actual current profile rather than relying only on component data-sheet values.

Manage Communication Energy

Communication can be one of the largest and least predictable energy consumers in a remote device. Network registration, signal acquisition, retransmissions and connection recovery may consume more energy than the payload transmission itself.

Firmware should therefore define retry limits, back-off intervals, connection timeouts and reporting policies. Where the application permits it, data can be grouped into an appropriate transmission window rather than initiating unnecessary communication sessions.

These decisions must not compromise required data availability or latency. The correct balance depends on the application and should be verified under representative network conditions.

Measure Power in Real Operating Conditions

A single sleep-current measurement is not enough to validate a long-life device. Testing should capture the complete current profile, including wake-up, sensing, processing, storage, communication, failed connection attempts and recovery.

The measured profile should be compared with the assumptions used in the battery-life estimate. Any difference should be investigated before the design is considered ready for deployment.

4. Design Communication Handling for Unreliable Networks

Remote embedded devices cannot assume that a communication network will always be available. Coverage may vary by location, time, antenna installation, building structure, network load and service-provider conditions.

Firmware should treat communication as an asynchronous and potentially unreliable service rather than a guaranteed operation.

Separate Measurement from Transmission

Measurement activities should not depend unnecessarily on immediate network availability. A device should be able to complete its required local functions and retain the resulting data even when a connection cannot be established.

This separation also prevents a slow or unavailable modem from blocking time-critical measurement or control functions.

Use Controlled Retry and Back-Off Policies

Repeated connection attempts at short intervals can waste energy and create excessive network activity. A retry policy should define the maximum number of attempts, the delay between attempts, any back-off strategy and the conditions for returning to the normal reporting schedule.

The policy should distinguish between temporary failures and persistent faults where practical. It should also prevent a failed communication task from keeping the entire device awake indefinitely.

Validate the Communication Result

A successful modem response does not always mean that application data has been received and accepted by the intended platform. Firmware should distinguish between network attachment, transport-level delivery and application-level acknowledgement where the protocol and system architecture support those distinctions.

Sequence numbers, message identifiers or other application mechanisms can help detect duplicate transmissions and support controlled recovery after interrupted sessions.

The exact approach depends on the communication protocol and backend design. The important requirement is to define what constitutes successful delivery and how the device behaves when that confirmation is absent.

5. Protect Data with Local Buffering and Integrity Checks

Long-life devices should be designed to preserve important information across communication outages and unexpected resets.

Local buffering allows measurement and event collection to continue when the network is unavailable. Once connectivity returns, stored records can be transmitted according to the defined recovery policy.

Define a Clear Data-Buffering Strategy

The design should specify:

  • How many records can be stored locally.
  • Which data has priority when storage capacity is limited.
  • Whether records are retained until confirmed delivery.
  • How duplicate transmission is identified and handled.
  • What happens when the buffer becomes full.
  • How records are ordered and timestamped after a prolonged outage.

Storage technology should be selected with its endurance, retention, write granularity, power-failure behaviour and capacity in mind. Frequent writes to non-volatile memory may create unnecessary wear, while a buffer that is too small may lose useful history during an extended outage.

Make Writes Recoverable

A power interruption can occur while data is being written. Firmware should use appropriate integrity checks and record formats so that incomplete or corrupted records can be detected during the next start-up.

Depending on the storage medium and risk level, techniques may include checksums or CRCs, sequence counters, record validity markers, redundant metadata and transactional update patterns.

The device should be able to distinguish valid records from incomplete writes rather than assuming that every stored byte represents a complete measurement.

6. Design Fault Detection and Recovery Deliberately

Long-life devices will encounter abnormal conditions during their service life. These may include sensor errors, communication timeouts, low supply voltage, unexpected resets, memory faults and peripheral lock-ups.

A robust firmware design does not assume that every operation will succeed. It defines how failures are detected, recorded and handled.

Use Appropriate Recovery Mechanisms

  • Watchdog supervision: Detect a firmware task or system state that has stopped making progress.
  • Timeouts: Prevent a peripheral or communication operation from waiting indefinitely.
  • Peripheral reinitialisation: Recover a sensor or communication interface where a controlled restart is appropriate.
  • Reset-cause recording: Preserve information that helps distinguish power-on resets, watchdog resets and other reset conditions.
  • Data validation: Check stored configuration and records before relying on them after a restart.
  • Safe operating state: Ensure that a failed subsystem does not cause unsafe or unpredictable behaviour elsewhere in the device.

Prevent Recovery Loops

Recovery itself consumes time and energy. A device that repeatedly resets a modem, scans for a network or restarts a failing peripheral without limits may enter a loop that drains its battery without restoring service.

Recovery policies should therefore be bounded. They may use increasing delays, attempt counters, a temporary degraded mode or a scheduled retry after a defined interval. Critical functions should remain available where possible while a non-essential subsystem is unavailable.

The objective is not to make every fault invisible. It is to keep the device in a known and controlled condition, preserve important information and provide enough diagnostic evidence to support investigation.

7. Plan Firmware Updates and Configuration for the Field

A device expected to remain deployed for many years may need firmware corrections, security updates, configuration changes or support for evolving backend requirements.

The update strategy should be considered early in the product architecture. Adding a field-update mechanism late in development can require changes to memory allocation, bootloader design, communication workflows and production procedures.

Protect the Update Process

A reliable update process should consider image integrity, authenticity, compatibility and interruption recovery. Depending on the product risk and threat model, this may involve cryptographic verification, signed firmware images, protected boot mechanisms and controlled authorisation of update commands.

The device should not activate an incomplete or unauthorised image. Where memory resources permit, a dual-image or recovery arrangement can reduce the risk of an interrupted update leaving the device unusable.

Manage Compatibility

Firmware updates must account for hardware revisions, bootloader versions, configuration formats and deployed device variants. A new firmware image should not assume that every device has identical memory, peripheral or board characteristics unless that assumption is enforced by the product design.

Version information should be available for the device hardware, firmware, bootloader and relevant configuration schema. This makes it easier to assess compatibility before an update is released.

Control Configuration Changes

Configuration changes can affect reporting intervals, measurement schedules, communication behaviour and power consumption. They should be validated, stored safely and traceable to an approved configuration version.

A configuration mechanism should also define how invalid values are rejected and how the device returns to a known configuration if an update is interrupted or fails validation.

8. Make Diagnostics and Maintainability Part of the Design

A device that operates for years without physical access still needs a way to communicate its health and operating history. Without useful diagnostics, a field issue may be difficult to distinguish from a network problem, battery limitation, sensor fault or software defect.

Diagnostic information should be selected carefully because storing and transmitting excessive logs can consume memory, energy and communication capacity.

Useful diagnostic items may include:

  • Firmware, bootloader and hardware revision identifiers.
  • Reset cause and restart counters.
  • Battery voltage or other available power-health indicators.
  • Communication attempt, failure and recovery counters.
  • Sensor error and measurement validation counters.
  • Local storage utilisation and buffer-overflow indicators.
  • Last successful reporting time and pending-record count.
  • Firmware update status and result.

Diagnostics should use consistent error codes and event definitions. Where a device has limited memory, counters and selected event records may provide more value than unrestricted text logging.

Maintain the Firmware as a Product Asset

Maintainability also depends on engineering discipline. Source code should be version-controlled, builds should be reproducible, release artefacts should be archived and changes should be traceable to requirements or defect records.

Hardware dependencies, communication interfaces, configuration parameters and recovery behaviour should be documented. These records become especially important when the original development team is no longer responsible for the product.

A long-life product should not depend on undocumented knowledge held by one engineer. Its firmware should remain understandable, testable and supportable throughout its intended service period.

9. Verify Firmware Against Long-Term Field Conditions

Functional testing confirms that the device performs its intended operations under defined test conditions. Long-life embedded products require additional verification to establish that those functions remain reliable under repeated cycles, interruptions and changing field conditions.

A practical verification programme should include several levels:

Functional and Interface Testing

Verify measurement accuracy, peripheral interfaces, timing, data formats, communication sequences and configuration behaviour against documented requirements.

Power-Profile Testing

Measure current during sleep, wake-up, measurement, processing, storage, network registration, transmission and fault recovery. Compare the measured profile with the battery-life assumptions.

Communication Resilience Testing

Test weak signal conditions, network unavailability, delayed responses, rejected messages, interrupted sessions and restoration of connectivity. Confirm that retries remain bounded and buffered data is handled correctly.

Fault-Injection and Power-Interruption Testing

Introduce sensor disconnection, peripheral non-response, low-voltage conditions, unexpected resets and power loss during storage or update operations. Verify that the device returns to a known state and that important records remain valid.

Long-Duration and Repeated-Cycle Testing

Run representative operating cycles over an extended period. Monitor memory use, counters, communication behaviour, unexpected resets and changes in current consumption. This can reveal resource leaks, counter overflows, timing drift and rare state-transition defects.

Acceptance criteria should be measurable. Statements such as "the device shall recover from communication failure" should be supported by defined test conditions, recovery limits, data-retention expectations and power-consumption constraints.

10. A Practical Firmware Design Review Checklist

Before releasing firmware for a long-life embedded product, engineering teams can use the following questions to review whether the design addresses the main lifecycle risks.

  • Are the device operating profile and service-life assumptions documented?
  • Are firmware modules, responsibilities and interfaces clearly defined?
  • Are operating states and state transitions explicit and testable?
  • Has the complete power profile been estimated and measured?
  • Are communication retries, timeouts and back-off policies bounded?
  • Can the device continue essential local functions during network outages?
  • Are buffered records protected against interruption and corruption?
  • Are watchdogs, reset causes and fault-recovery paths properly implemented?
  • Is the update mechanism secure and resilient to interruption?
  • Are hardware, firmware and configuration versions traceable?
  • Can field diagnostics support investigation without excessive overhead?
  • Have long-duration, power-interruption and communication-resilience tests been completed?

Conclusion: Design for the Years After Deployment

Long-life embedded firmware should be judged not only by whether it works during initial testing, but by how predictably it behaves throughout years of field operation.

A suitable architecture, disciplined power management, resilient communication handling, protected local data, bounded fault recovery and maintainable update mechanisms all contribute to that outcome. None of these areas can be treated as an isolated feature because each affects the device's overall reliability and service life.

The most effective approach is to translate field operating requirements into explicit firmware behaviours, validate those behaviours under realistic conditions and preserve the engineering information needed to maintain the product over time.

Long-life firmware is not simply firmware that runs for a long time. It is firmware designed to remain reliable, recoverable and supportable for the full period the device is expected to serve.
